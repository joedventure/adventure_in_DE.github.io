## City History of Paderborn

Paderborn derives its name from the Pader River, which originates in the city center. As early as the Stone Age, humans were already living along the banks of the Pader River. However, what truly gave Paderborn its historical significance was the year 777 AD, when Charlemagne, King of the Franks, held an Imperial Diet (Reichstag) on Saxon soil for the first time. This also marks the earliest official written record of Paderborn. After enduring numerous Saxon uprisings and wars, Paderborn was officially incorporated into the Frankish Realm.

Paderborn played an extremely crucial role in European religious and political history. In 799 AD, Pope Leo III fled to Paderborn to seek protection from Charlemagne following riots in Rome. In return, Charlemagne was crowned Emperor at St. Peter's Basilica in Rome on December 25, 800 AD. In the same year, the Bishopric of Paderborn was officially established, and the Paderborn Cathedral was built accordingly.

Around 1000 AD, Paderborn obtained city rights, and in 1295, it became a member of the Hanseatic League (Hansebund). In the 14th century, Paderborn became a Prince-Bishopric (Fürstbistum). Due to the raging Black Death at the time, the ruling Prince-Bishop Balduin von Steinfurt moved his residence to Schloss Neuhaus. In terms of educational development, the first university (a theological faculty) in the Westphalia region was established in Paderborn in 1614.

In 1802, Paderborn fell into Prussian hands, though it was briefly incorporated into Napoleon's Kingdom of Westphalia from 1807 to 1813. Following the Congress of Vienna, Paderborn reverted to Prussia and became a district seat in 1816. The mid-to-late 19th century saw significant developments in local transportation and military presence, such as the railway connecting Paderborn and Hamm in 1850 (one of the oldest railway lines in Germany) and the troop training grounds in Paderborn-Sennelager.

During the Weimar Republic, the first local branch of the Nazi Party was established here, growing from a small party to gradually gaining widespread support. After Hitler came to power in 1933, he and the Imperial President Hindenburg were even granted honorary citizenship of Paderborn. Like many towns at the time, Jewish residents faced continuous persecution, and others could not escape the disaster brought by World War II. By the end of WWII, approximately 85% of the buildings in Paderborn's city center had been completely destroyed, and the city's population plummeted to just over 20,000.

After the war, Paderborn was assigned to the British occupation zone and embarked on a long process of reconstruction. During this period, the University of Paderborn was founded. The administrative reorganization in 1975 pushed Paderborn's population past the 100,000 mark, officially elevating it to the status of a major city and a vibrant university town.

## Paderborn Cathedral (Paderborner Dom)
![Paderborner Dom]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/PB-Dom.jpg)
![Die drei Hasen]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Hase.jpg)
![Die drei Hasen2]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Hase-2.jpg)

As early as AD 799, when Pope Leo III met with Charlemagne, King of the Franks, at the source of the Pader River, a grand church was already built on this site. In AD 836, to convert the Saxons of that era, the second bishop, Badurad, brought the relics of Saint Liborius all the way from Le Mans, France, using this occasion to greatly expand the cathedral. This religious bond led the two cities to become sister cities, establishing a cross-border friendship that has lasted over a millennium.

The most eye-catching feature of Paderborn Cathedral is undoubtedly its grand 93-meter-high west tower. Blending Romanesque and Gothic architectural styles, this tower serves as the icon of Paderborn. The Cathedral's Portal of Paradise (Paradiesportal) is equally unmissable. Its exquisite arched carvings depict the Apostles and Saint Julian, the first bishop of Le Mans, vividly showcasing the deep historical connection between Paderborn and Le Mans. Step down into the crypt to find one of the largest underground crypts in Germany. Virtually unchanged since its renovation in AD 1100, it houses the relics of Saint Liborius, the patron saint of the city.

Legend has it that when believers escorted the holy relics from France to Germany, a peacock led the procession the entire way until they reached Paderborn Cathedral. This is why peacock motifs can be seen everywhere throughout Paderborn. The Libori festival, held every July, also originates from celebrating the arrival of the holy relics in Paderborn. Today, it has evolved into a massive, multi-day celebration combining religious processions, funfairs, and traditional markets, drawing over a million visitors each year.

Hidden within the cloister of Paderborn Cathedral lies a low-key yet captivating masterpiece of art: the Window of Three Rabbits (Drei-Hasen-Fenster).

Crafted in the early 16th century, this masterpiece was meticulously carved from red sandstone. Although modest in size, its fascinating charm lies in its ingenious geometric arrangement. Looking closely, you will notice only three ears in total, yet no matter which rabbit you focus on, each one appears to have two complete ears! This design perfectly echoes the famous local doggerel: "Three rabbits and three ears, yet every rabbit has two" (Der Hasen und der Löffel drei, und doch hat jeder Hase zwei).

These three rabbits running in a circle are more than just a clever piece of craftsmanship; they carry deep cultural significance. In antiquity, rabbits—known for their agility and rapid reproduction—were seen as symbols of vitality and life force. By the Middle Ages, the motif of three rabbits sharing ears took on a religious meaning, serving to visualize the concept of the Holy Trinity. All in all, this window is an absolute must-see landmark when visiting Paderborn.

## Paderborn Town Hall (Rathaus Paderborn)
![PB-Rathaus]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/PB-Rathaus.jpg)

As a landmark as famous as the Cathedral, Paderborn Town Hall is a prime example of Weser Renaissance (Weserrenaissance) architecture in Germany.

The history of the town hall traces back to 1279. The current building was ordered by the Prince-Bishop in 1611 and rebuilt by the architect Hermann Baumhauer between 1613 and 1620 in the Weser Renaissance style on the foundation of the old structure. In the following centuries, the town hall served various purposes, including a customs office, police station, and warehouse. During major interior renovations in the 1870s, the architect in charge had a dispute with the city government over budget overruns and was replaced; in protest, he angrily carved a malicious inscription in a hidden spot (which is why I couldn't find it either), parts of which survive to this day. In 1945 during WWII, the town hall was devastated by fire in an air raid, leaving only the outer walls; the precious historical interior decorations and the astronomical clock were destroyed in the blaze. Reconstruction efforts began after the war and continued until 1958, during which a lottery even had to be issued to raise funds for the interior decoration.

The most eye-catching feature of the building itself is its ornate gables, consisting of a main gable and two smaller projecting gables on the sides. The ground floor features two open halls supported by Doric columns, which served as a courtroom in the past, while the outer walls of the main floor are almost entirely replaced by dense rows of windows, creating a striking visual effect. Additionally, a Baroque fountain decorated with Paderborn's coat of arms stands directly in front of the town hall, adding a wonderful highlight to the overall scenery.

## Market Church (Marktkirche)
![PB-Marktkirche]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/PB-Marktkirche.jpg)

The Paderborn Market Church is one of the most iconic Baroque buildings in the city. Originally a Jesuit church sponsored by Prince-Bishop Ferdinand von Fürstenberg, construction began in 1678. The main structure was completed in 1686, but the interior Baroque decorations continued until 1730, when the organ was completed.

Entering the church, you can see the magnificent, golden high altar. In fact, the original main altar was destroyed in WWII. Later, driven by a local association and funded jointly by the state of North Rhine-Westphalia and the city of Paderborn, it took over a decade to reconstruct based on old photos and sketches, standing today as a masterpiece of art worth admiring. Besides this, Jesuit monograms such as IHS (Jesus), MRA (Maria), ISP (Joseph), IGN (Ignatius, founder of the order), and XAV (St. Francis Xavier, patron saint of the church) are scattered throughout the church, representing the historical roots of the Market Church.

## Pader Springs (Paderquellen)
![PB-Paderquellen]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Paderquellen.jpg)

The Pader River originates in Paderborn, with its headwaters formed by more than 200 springs, gushing out up to 5,000 liters of water per second on average. However, the river's course is extremely short—only 4.6 kilometers—making it the shortest river in Germany.

This unique hydrological landscape stems from the local geological structure. When rainfall from the surrounding plateau seeps underground, it is blocked by impermeable marl stone, eventually emerging en masse at fault lines in the city area. Because the underground water flows extremely fast, it takes only two to four days after rain for the water to reach the spring outlets, which causes the springs to often become turbid with silt after heavy rainfall.

Since ancient times, the Pader River has been inseparable from the lives of local residents and the city's development. Abundant water resources provided a stable foundation for local mills and brewing industries, and today, the spring parks within the city serve as great spots for people to relax and unwind.

## Neuhaus Castle (Schloss Neuhaus)

Located a short distance northwest of Paderborn, Schloss Neuhaus is surrounded by rivers and boasts a wonderful environment.

The castle originally served as a manor for the Bishop of Paderborn (appearing in documents in 1036). Later, after a dispute with the citizens, the bishop decided to move his residence here and constructed the Gothic Haus Spiegel in 1370. In the early 16th century, architect Jörg Unkair built the Weser Renaissance-style Haus Braunschweig, introducing this brand-new architectural style to the region. After the four iconic round corner towers were completed between 1589 and 1597, the castle's square layout as we see it today was officially finalized. With the secularization of church property in 1802, the castle was converted into a Prussian prison and garrison, used by various military units until the end of WWII. After being acquired by the city government in the 1960s, most of the castle's space was repurposed into a secondary school, which continues to this day. Imagine taking classes here—it sounds pretty cool!

## Card Game "Sechsundsechzig" (Sixty-Six)
![66]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/66.jpg)

Have you ever heard of a classic German two-player card game called 66 (Sechsundsechzig)? According to legend, 66 was invented in 1652 in a tavern located at Eckkamp 66 in Paderborn. Although some point out that this house number didn't even exist at the time, and that the game actually first appeared in an 1813 decree in Coburg—evolving from another ancient game called "Wedding"—a commemorative plaque is mounted on the outer wall here today to commemorate this historical event.

Although I haven't played it either, the core concept of the game is to accumulate 66 points from a deck of playing cards to win, which is very simple. I guess if this game were invented in 2025, it would probably be called 67 instead 💀

## Wewelsburg
![Wewelsburg1]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Wewelsburg-1.jpg)
![Wewelsburg2]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Wewelsburg-2.jpg)
![Wewelsburg3]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Paderborn/Wewelsburg-3.jpg)

Wewelsburg is very special, not only because of its unique triangular structure, but also because of the dark history that once took place here.

Located near Paderborn, Wewelsburg was already mentioned in the 10th century. The castle structure we see today only began construction in the early 16th century by the Bishop of Paderborn. The floor plan of the castle forms an isosceles triangle with a tower at each corner, a rare design among German castles. After being damaged during the Thirty Years' War, the castle fell into a long period of neglect until 1924, when ownership was transferred to the local government, and it was restored to serve as a local history museum and youth hostel.

The global Great Depression in 1929 completely altered the fate of this castle. Due to financial difficulties, the local government could not afford the maintenance costs and urgently needed someone to take it over. Right at this time, SS leader Heinrich Himmler was searching for a suitable site as an organizational center. Because eastern Westphalia was a Germanic heartland long ago, and this area was the ancient battlefield where Arminius led Germanic tribes to defeat the Roman legions, it held strong symbolic value for the occult-obsessed Himmler. After his first visit in 1933, he signed a symbolic lease agreement with an annual rent of one Reichsmark.

It was initially planned as an SS training center, but no training was ever held here; instead, it gradually transformed into a meeting place and ideological sanctuary for high-ranking SS officers (SS-Obergruppenführer). They would gather here annually, and after their death, their SS Honor Rings (SS-Ehrenring; also known as the Totenkopfring), symbolizing their status, were preserved here.

To enhance the castle's solemnity, Himmler removed the outer wall plaster to expose the original stone and added pseudo-medieval decorations to make the entire castle look more historical. The North Tower was transformed into an occult-infused core space, with the basement acting as a crypt for honoring deceased high-ranking leaders, and the ground floor featuring a marble hall with a Black Sun totem. All these construction projects were completed through forced labor by prisoners from the Niederhagen concentration camp under inhumane conditions. Statistics show that about one-third of the prisoners died under this brutal policy.

After the Battle of Stalingrad in 1943, the military tide turned against Germany, and Himmler began planning how to negotiate with the Allies. In 1945, he ordered Wewelsburg to be blown up to destroy all traces of what he had done there. However, due to a shortage of explosives, Wewelsburg was only partially damaged. The castle and the Niederhagen concentration camp were subsequently liberated by American forces.

After the war, Wewelsburg was rapidly rebuilt, and the main body of the castle resumed its functions as a museum and youth hostel. Permanent exhibitions display the ideology and terror atrocities of the SS, allowing us to gain a deeper understanding and reflect on what happened during this period. As for the space in the North Tower, the museum placed colorful beanbag chairs on top of the Sun Wheel totem for visitors to rest on, thereby "desacralizing" it and breaking the original cultish ritual atmosphere. In the surrounding villages, the former concentration camp was transformed into public facilities (such as a fire department), and a simple triangular monument was erected to remember history and honor the victims.
