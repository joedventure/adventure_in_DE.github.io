## City History of Altenburg

![ABG-Marktplatz]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-Marktplatz.jpg)

Located in central Germany, Altenburg is a charming city with over 1,050 years of history, weaving together the glory of the Holy Roman Empire with rich folk culture. As early as AD 976, the city's name appeared in documents during the reign of Holy Roman Emperor Otto II, foreshadowing its remarkable historical status.

The rise of Altenburg was largely due to its advantageous geographical location. In the Middle Ages, it was situated right next to the vital long-distance trade routes, Via Imperii and Via Regia. Massive merchant caravans and logistics moved along the Via Imperii connecting Leipzig and Nuremberg, forming a prosperous commercial settlement in the early old market area known as "Brühl." Many craftsmen—such as blacksmiths, coopers, and ropemakers—settled here, laying a solid economic foundation for the city. With its rapid development, documents as early as 1192 mentioned a new market (novum forum), demonstrating how fast it was expanding. By 1256, Altenburg's city privileges were confirmed, with regulations even requiring that grain, honey, and wool sold in the market be traded using Altenburg currency, proving that it was undoubtedly a premier medieval metropolis at the time.

Speaking of Altenburg's golden age, one cannot omit the famous Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I "Barbarossa". Barbarossa was exceptionally fond of this place, establishing an Imperial Palace (Kaiserpfalz) and visiting seven times between 1165 and 1188. The emperor's presence brought not only political influence but also tangible architectural legacy, such as the twin brick towers known as the Red Spire (Rote Spitzen), which remain Altenburg's most iconic landmark today.

Altenburg also holds cultural significance. In 1519, Martin Luther preached at the local St. Bartholomew's Church and defended his theological views. Furthermore, Altenburg boasts over 400 years of playing card manufacturing history. Between 1810 and 1815, locals invented Skat, a card game that later became Germany's national card game and swept across the entire country. To commemorate this fun invention, a Skat Fountain (Skatbrunnen) was specifically erected in the city.

### Kunz and the Altenburg Prinzenraub

Throughout Altenburg's centuries of history, a highly dramatic kidnapping once took place. The protagonist was the battle-tested knight Kunz von Kaufungen. He had fought valiantly for Elector Frederick II of Saxony in the Saxon Fratricidal War (Sächsischer Bruderkrieg; 1446–1451). However, during the war, Kunz not only suffered devastating loss of property but was also captured by Bohemian forces, having to pay a huge ransom out of his own pocket to regain freedom. After the war, he hoped the Elector would compensate his massive losses in recognition of his past contributions. Facing a financial crisis himself, Frederick II coldly refused, viewing Kunz merely as a hired soldier with no obligation for his losses to be reimbursed.

Left with no recourse, an enraged Kunz ultimately resorted to extreme retaliation. On July 7, 1455, he boldly sneaked into Altenburg Castle and kidnapped Prince Ernst and Prince Albrecht, the two sons of Frederick II. This event became known in history as the Altenburg Prinzenraub (Kidnapping of the Altenburg Princes). However, this revenge plan went terribly wrong. On the night of the kidnapping, Prince Albrecht was luckily rescued by a charcoal burner, and Kunz was subdued and arrested on the spot. Four days later, Kunz's accomplices voluntarily released Prince Ernst in exchange for a pardon. Ultimately, this farce ended with Kunz being legally beheaded in Freiberg on July 14 of the same year. Although the kidnapping failed, the two princes who survived the ordeal accomplished great deeds later in life, becoming the common ancestors of the two main branches of the House of Wettin and adding an indelible legendary stroke to the castle's history.


## The Card Game Skat

In addition to Sechsundsechzig mentioned earlier, another card game from Germany is extremely famous: Skat, which originated in Altenburg.

Regarding the birth of Skat, two interesting stories circulate. One claims it was invented by high-society figures during gatherings, but another more story-like account—accepted by most—is that a coachman brought back a three-player game called "Wendischer Schafkopf" from the Ore Mountains (Erzgebirge). He introduced it to his card-loving employer, which immediately sparked a craze and quickly took the entire city by storm.

In fact, Skat's rules mix elements from various card games: based on Schafkopf, borrowing the concept of keeping two face-down cards from Tarot, and integrating the bidding mechanism of "L'Hombre", eventually evolving into this classic game requiring three players and a 32-card deck.

As Skat spread rapidly among university students and German-speaking regions, diverse local custom rules emerged everywhere. To resolve these headache-inducing rule disputes, over a thousand players gathered in Altenburg in 1886 to formulate the General German Skat Rules, initiating the tradition of Skat congresses. By 1899, the German Skat Association was officially established, marking the game's shift toward high organization.

Skat is not only popular entertainment but also deeply ingrained in German history and cultural development. More than two centuries after its invention, it was officially inscribed on UNESCO's German Intangible Cultural Heritage list at the end of 2016.

## Red Spire (Rote Spitzen)
![Rote Spitzen]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-Rote Spitzen.jpg)

Visiting Altenburg, it is hard not to be captivated by the landmark Red Spire. These twin red-brick towers were part of the St. Marien Monastery complex, built around 1165 under the patronage of Holy Roman Emperor Frederick I. Because Frederick I frequently visited northern Italy, the style of this church was deeply influenced by Lombardic cities like Milan and Pavia, making it one of the earliest large brick structures north of the Alps.

Following the wave of the Reformation in 1543, the monastery was dissolved and subsequently used for various purposes, including an orphanage, a prison, and even a psychiatric facility. It was not until 2006, when the City of Altenburg initiated a comprehensive monument preservation and restoration project, that this weather-beaten building was given new life. During the restoration, experts not only rediscovered a precious fresco depicting the Coronation of Mary by Christ in the apse of the south tower chapel—long sealed off by walls and considered a unique artistic treasure north of the Alps—but also found remnants of biblical frescoes in the vaulted arcades connecting the twin towers.

In addition to these archaeological discoveries, local folklore surrounding the Red Spire adds a touch of mystery. Legend has it that in 1160, Frederick I was penalized by the Pope and, to gain absolution, was forced to pledge the construction of 12 monasteries, the Red Spire being one of them. Interestingly, the tale goes that the emperor struggled to find suitable architects and stonemasons, so he had to design it himself. While pondering the exterior, he looked at his signature long-and-short red beard, got an inspiration, and ordered the two brick towers to be built at different heights.

As for how the Red Spire has stood firm for over 800 years, another local secret circulates. It is said that the year the towers were built coincided with a bumper grape harvest, making wine even cheaper than water. Thus, the craftsmen extravagantly used wine instead of water to mix the building mortar. Locals firmly believe that it was this rich wine that made the structure indestructible to this day.

## Altenburg Residential Castle (Residenzschloss Altenburg)
![Schloss1]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-Schloss-1.jpg)
![Schloss1]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-Schloss-2.jpg)

As the location of the Imperial Palace, the importance of Altenburg Castle goes without saying. Here, Barbarossa officially granted rule over the Duchy of Bavaria to the House of Wittelsbach, disciplined his arch-enemy Henry the Lion of the House of Welf, and deprived him of his territories. From receiving the fief in 1180, the Wittelsbach family ruled Bavaria until the end of World War I, establishing Bavaria's profound family cultural identity and maintaining a unique political standing and cultural identity among German states.

Following the Battle of Lucka in 1307, the territory and castle of Altenburg fell into the hands of the House of Wettin. From then on, the castle served as a long-term residence and palace for the Wettin family and later the Dukes of Saxe-Altenburg. The previously mentioned kidnapping of the Altenburg princes also took place here.

Beyond its history, the castle brings together architectural highlights from the Medieval, Renaissance, and Baroque periods. Inside lies a world-famous playing card museum showcasing traditional card-making workshops, ancient weaponry, paintings, and even a precious East Asian porcelain collection. Furthermore, the expansive castle gardens vividly reflect the elegant court life of past eras.

## St. Bartholomew's Church (St. Bartholomäi)
![St. Bartholomäi]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-St. Bartholomäi.jpg)

When visiting Altenburg, it is difficult not to be drawn to the charm of St. Bartholomew's Church. It is not only the city's oldest church but also an iconic witness to the German Reformation. Built around AD 1100, St. Bartholomew's was originally funded by local merchants as a Romanesque church. Unfortunately, it suffered severe damage from the Hussites around 1430; however, this paved the way for it to be gradually rebuilt into a Gothic church over the following decades, complete with stunning stellar vaulting and vivid wall paintings. The church originally had twin towers, but the north tower collapsed in the 1660s, and the south tower was subsequently dismantled, eventually replaced by the square Baroque bell tower we see today.

St. Bartholomew's Church also holds a place in the history of the Reformation. Martin Luther visited Altenburg multiple times and preached in this very church. In addition, he officiated the wedding of his classmate Wenzeslaus Linck here. The union of a Protestant pastor and a local citizen's daughter was a significant milestone for the Reformation at the time, and locals reportedly celebrated for two days. Furthermore, Luther's close ally and helmsman of the Reformation, Georg Spalatin, served as the pastor of Altenburg from 1525 until his death and was buried here. A marble tablet inside and a life-sized statue at the entrance commemorate his dedication.

## Brethren Church (Brüderkirche)
![Brüderkirche]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Altenburg/ABG-Brüderkirche.jpg)

Seeing the Brüderkirche in Altenburg for the first time was truly striking. Although also built of red brick, perhaps due to the lighting, the red hue felt exceptionally vibrant—distinctly standing out compared to typical red-brick churches in Northern Germany. The mosaic depicting the "Sermon on the Mount" (Bergpredigt) on the exterior wall was also captivating. Its gleaming gold decorations brought the image to life, making sunlight appear to radiate outward from Jesus.

The interior features a semi-circular layout, allowing every visitor a direct view of the altar. The interior walls are based on white, adorned with exquisite Medieval and Art Nouveau patterns, very different from many churches visited before. The western wall features a large golden-background fresco vividly illustrating the Nativity, Crucifixion, and Resurrection of Jesus. Furthermore, stained glass windows depict stories of the Reformation, and above the organ, portraits of the musician J.S. Bach, Martin Luther, theologian Paul Gerhardt, and King Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden can be seen. Regrettably, an event was taking place inside during the visit, preventing entry, so one could only rely on other photos.

As for its origins, the Brüderkirche was initially a 13th-century Franciscan monastery. Since Franciscan friars were known as "Lesser Brothers" (Mindere Brüder), the church earned the name Brüderkirche (Brethren Church), which remains in use today. The monastery was closed during the Reformation in 1529 and later transformed into a parish church. Between 1627 and 1675, it served as a ducal burial church. Records state that between 1501 and 1512, Elector Frederick the Wise (Kurfürst Friedrich der Weise) ordered the construction of a late Baroque-style church on the site. In 1901, the old Franciscan church was demolished due to dilapidation. The current structure was designed by Berlin architect Jürgen Kröger and consecrated on May 7, 1905.
