## History of Erfurt
![Erfurt1]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Cover.jpg)
![Erfurt2]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Cover2.jpg)

### Origins of Erfurt

Erfurt possesses an extraordinarily rich historical legacy. The surrounding area is an ancient settling ground, with human activity dating back over 100,000 years to the Paleolithic era. Between the 1st and 4th centuries AD, the Hermunduri, a Germanic tribe, began settling here. Taking advantage of the ford along the Gera River, they established early settlements near ancient transport routes (located directly beneath several modern-day city streets), before gradually assimilating into the Thuringian tribe.

The name of the Thuringians (Thoringi) first appeared in records around 400 AD. By the turn of the next century, they formed a powerful Thuringian Kingdom, reaching its peak under King Herminafrid. However, this prosperity was short-lived. Between 531 and 534 AD, the Franks conquered the region by force, completely destroying the Thuringian Kingdom, which subsequently became the eastern frontier of the Frankish realm.

Erfurt was first mentioned in historical documents in 742 AD. Archbishop Boniface wrote a letter to the Pope requesting confirmation for three bishoprics he had established, one of which was located at a place called "Erphesfurt" (the other two being Würzburg and Büraburg). In his letter, Boniface described it as an "already long-existing fortified settlement of pagan peasants." The following year, the Pope officially confirmed the legitimacy of these bishoprics. Although around 747 the Diocese of Erfurt was absorbed into the jurisdiction of the Archbishop of Mainz, Erfurt retained its status as the religious and political heart of the Thuringia region.

### Strategic Role and Early Urbanization

In the 9th century, Erfurt became a crucial eastern frontier outpost for the Carolingian dynasty in the East Frankish Kingdom. A document from 802 AD explicitly records the presence of a royal palace (Königspfalz) here, likely situated on Petersberg. According to records, Charlemagne stayed here and issued the Diedenhofen Capitulary (Diedenhofener Kapitular) in 805, officially declaring Erfurt a key trading outpost on the eastern border of the Frankish Empire. This decree mandated that merchants traveling to Slavic (Slawen) and Avar (Awaren) territories undergo strict supervision by imperial officials here, while strictly prohibiting the sale of Western weapons to these peoples. Situated on the ancient trade route connecting the Rhine-Main region with the Thuringian heartland, this policy greatly stimulated the growth of long-distance commerce. Following the Treaty of Verdun in 843 AD, Erfurt's strategic value within East Francia continued to rise, making it a favored residence for kings and emperors—exemplified by the Imperial Diet held here by Louis the German, King of East Francia, who also successfully led forces to defend against eastern border threats.

By the second half of the 9th century, the dispersed settlements of Erfurt began merging, gradually developing into an early urban center. In addition to local merchants, an increasing number of long-distance traders chose to settle here, while artisans providing goods and services for the market also began to gather.

### The Empire of Blue Gold: Medieval Prosperity and Expansion

Medieval Erfurt experienced remarkable growth across all spheres. Economically, Erfurt grew into a thriving metropolis on the back of its lucrative dye trade. From the 13th century onward, it developed into one of the largest woad (Waid) markets in the Holy Roman Empire. Around 300 surrounding villages cultivated this plant extensively; a document from 1579 records 49 villages under Erfurt's jurisdiction engaged in woad farming. The highly profitable blue dye extracted from woad was exported in massive quantities to European textile centers such as Nuremberg, Lübeck, and Flanders.

To safeguard this economic lifeline, the city council established a strict market monopoly in the 1351 Erfurt Market Regulations (Erfurter Zuchtbrief). All woad transactions were strictly confined to Erfurt's woad market (located at Anger Square) between Trinity Sunday (the Sunday after Pentecost/Pfingsten) and the autumn equinox, with the ringing of the Waidglocke (woad bell) serving as the official signal to open the market.

Furthermore, in 1331, Emperor Ludwig IV renewed Erfurt's trade fair privileges, transforming it into a commercial and long-distance trade hub with European influence. Accompanied by growing wealth, the city joined the Goslar Alliance of Hanseatic Cities (Goslarer Bund von Hansestädten) in 1430, elevating its commercial and political clout to its peak. Although Erfurt's economy later declined due to conflicts with surrounding territories, the woad trade reached a second peak in the second half of the 16th century. This economic resurgence ignited a wave of Renaissance architecture, giving rise to magnificent buildings such as "Zum Roten Ochsen" (Red Ox) and "Zum Breiten Herd" (Broad Hearth) on the Fish Market (Fischmarkt), as well as "Zum Stockfisch" (Stockfish) on Johannesstraße.

To protect its hard-won commercial wealth and trade routes against military threats from neighboring lords, Erfurt pursued an active policy of alliances and territorial expansion. Between 1304 and 1306, Erfurt, Mühlhausen, and Nordhausen formally established the Thuringian Tri-City Alliance (Thüringer Dreistädtebund). For nearly 180 years, these three cities effectively preserved regional peace and urban autonomy through mutual military defense and trade route protection, proving to be an undeniable political force in central Germany.

Concurrently, Erfurt expanded its influence by purchasing surrounding territories, obtaining the imperial fief of Kapellendorf (Reichslehen Kapellendorf) bestowed by King Karl IV in 1352. This gave Erfurt the special status of an immediate imperial fief, along with coining rights and representation at the Imperial Diet. Coins minted during this era deliberately combined the Wheel of Mainz (representing its liege lord) with the newly acquired coat of arms of Kapellendorf, demonstrating its near-independent political standing to all of Europe. Erfurt's territorial expansion peaked around 1470, controlling roughly a hundred surrounding villages and castles.

### From Imperial Seat of Learning to Cradle of the Reformation

Underpinned by strong economic power, Erfurt emerged as a major European academic center. After securing papal approval from Pope Clement VII in 1379, the University of Erfurt officially opened in 1392 with all four faculties: theology, law, medicine, and liberal arts. It was the fifth-oldest institution of higher learning in the Holy Roman Empire. It is believed that Johannes Gutenberg, inventor of movable metal type printing, studied here in 1418 under the name Johannes de Altavilla. Regrettably, this centuries-old university was ordered closed by the Prussian government in 1816, and only reopened in 1994 after an interruption of nearly 180 years.

Beyond Gutenberg, Martin Luther was also a notable alumnus of the University of Erfurt. He matriculated in 1501, earned his Master of Arts four years later, and entered the Augustinian Monastery to pursue monastic life. In April 1521, on his way to the Diet of Worms, Luther passed through Erfurt to a enthusiastic reception and preached to large crowds at the Augustinian Monastery. In June of the same year, inspired by new ideas, students, citizens, and surrounding peasants initiated the "Pfaffensturm" (Priests' Storm), destroying over 40 Catholic Church properties and clerical residences over three consecutive days. During the German Peasants' War of 1525, about a thousand peasants flooded into Erfurt, dismantling the administration of the Archbishop of Mainz and demanding reform. This ultimately led to a compromise in the 1530 Treaty of Hammelburg (Vertrag von Hammelburg): the Cathedral and 8 other churches remained Catholic, while the rest were allocated to Protestants, establishing a state of religious coexistence.

### 1184: The Most Absurd Imperial Diet in History

Medieval Erfurt was also the stage for one of the most absurd tragedies in history: the Latrine Disaster of Erfurt (Latrinensturz).

On July 26, 1184, the future Holy Roman Emperor Henry VI personally visited the city to mediate a bitter, longstanding feud between the Archbishop of Mainz and Landgrave Ludwig III of Thuringia. To attend this crucial arbitration, the most prominent counts and bishops from across the empire arrived in fine attire, packing the assembly hall to capacity. However, everyone overlooked the fact that the floor of this ancient wooden structure could not support the immense weight of over a hundred fully armored noblemen.

In an instant, the wooden floor collapsed with a deafening crash. To make matters worse, directly below the hall lay not an ordinary story, but a foul, deep latrine pit. Dozens of proud nobles plummeted straight into the unimaginable abyss. Weighted down by heavy metal armor, those who fell could not climb out and ultimately suffered the humiliating fate of drowning in sewage. Records indicate that approximately 60 nobles lost their lives that day in a horrifying scene.

Meanwhile, King Henry VI and the Archbishop of Mainz happened to be sitting on a stone window ledge in a corner of the hall. Watching the wooden floor vanish beneath them in terror, they clung desperately to the window frame until rescuers raised ladders to extract them. Landgrave Ludwig III also miraculously survived. The mediation, originally intended to resolve disputes through peaceful means, ultimately resolved the issue in the most brutal way imaginable—because many of the nobles involved in the feud were permanently buried in that indescribable pit.

### The Fatal Loop Between Erfurt and Mainz

Erfurt's political destiny remained inextricably intertwined with the Archbishops of Mainz. Around 1000 AD, following the Ottonian church reforms, secular rule over Erfurt was granted to the Archbishop of Mainz. However, as the burgher class rose, conflicts over civic autonomy erupted frequently. In 1212, 23 citizens appeared as witnesses in documents for the first time, marking the birth of an early city council, which officially functioned as an autonomous body by 1234. Conflicts reached a climax in 1279 when citizens forcibly expelled the Archbishop's officials, resulting in the city being slapped with a two-and-a-half-year papal interdict (Interdikt), which was only lifted in 1282 after the payment of a massive indemnity.

Although the city council bought jurisdiction rights in 1299 to consolidate judicial independence, the city's prosperity faced a harsh turning point in the late 15th century. In 1472, a devastating fire reduced nearly half the city to ashes, leaving behind a crushing financial burden for reconstruction. Simultaneously, long-distance trade routes shifted, and rival Leipzig obtained imperial fair privileges, severely stripping Erfurt of its advantages as a commercial hub. Under this double blow and facing increasingly powerful external forces, debt-ridden Erfurt was forced to compromise. The 1483 Treaties of Amorbach and Weimar forced the city to pay huge indemnities to Mainz and Saxony and acknowledge dependence on Mainz, severely undermining civic independence. In 1497, the city was coerced into signing an agreement explicitly recognizing the Archbishop of Mainz as its hereditary lord (Stat Erffurt Erbherrn).

In a desperate attempt to salvage its finances, the city council mortgaged the imperial fief of Kapellendorf—which granted the city its special status—to Saxony in 1507, never to redeem it. Yet this saved nothing, and the city council was ultimately forced to declare bankruptcy. External loss of sovereignty combined with internal financial collapse became the final straw for the public's trust, leading opposition factions to target the council that had long monopolized power. Between 1509 and 1510, accumulated civil unrest erupted into a bloody uprising known as the "Mad Year" (Tolles Jahr). During this turmoil, citizens fiercely resisted oligarchical rule, ousted wealthy patricians from the council, and on June 28, 1510, publicly executed high official Heinrich Kellner. This uprising forced a restructuring of power and sparked efforts toward institutional reform.

Yet these reforms could not reverse the city's subservient fate. In April 1618, Erfurt was forced to sign a final treaty with the Archbishop of Mainz, formally recognizing the city as property of the Electorate and renouncing all claims to imperial immediacy. The outbreak of the Thirty Years' War shortly thereafter devastated the city even further.

In October 1631, troops under King Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden entered Erfurt, transforming it into a formidable Swedish military stronghold. In 1632, the Swedish king transferred all rights of the Archbishop of Mainz (including subject villages, monasteries, and churches) to the city council. However, this gift was short-lived; under the Peace of Prague in 1635, the city was forced to return them. Swedish troops reoccupied the city from 1637 until 1650. When the Peace of Westphalia was signed in 1648, citizens' hopes of becoming a Free Imperial City were completely shattered. The 1650 treaty relentlessly restored all pre-1618 rights of the Archbishop of Mainz over Erfurt.

Exhausted by the Thirty Years' War and suffering total diplomatic defeat, the centuries-long power struggle between the two sides culminated in a violent, destructive showdown. In November 1663, the Archbishop's forces marched on the city, capturing two Erfurt citizens outside the walls and brutally torturing them to death. Enraged, citizens formed a militia to repel Mainz troops and launched an uprising.

Faced with stubborn resistance, Elector Johann Philipp von Schönborn of Mainz allied with French forces in October 1664 to capture Erfurt by force. The citizens and the entire city council were forced to swear allegiance to the Archbishop of Mainz. To permanently extinguish the centuries-old spirit of resistance and desire for autonomy, Mainz rulers began constructing the massive, stern Petersberg Citadel (Zitadelle Petersberg) on Petersberg hill overlooking the city in 1665. The cannons of this fortress were pointed not outward for defense, but directly at the city center. From then on, this once-proud city was stripped of most of its powers, reduced to a mere vassal of Mainz.

### Modern Evolution of Erfurt

Erfurt's modern political landscape underwent frequent shifts. In 1802, the city was formally incorporated into the Kingdom of Prussia. Just four years later, Prussia suffered a crushing defeat at the Battles of Jena and Auerstedt in October 1806, and the Erfurt fortress surrendered to French forces without resistance. The following year, Napoleon issued a decree declaring Erfurt and its surrounding areas an imperial domain under his personal authority (domaine réservé à l'Empereur).

In the autumn of 1808, Napoleon chose Erfurt as the stage to showcase his imperial authority. From September 27 to October 14, the famous Congress of Erfurt (Fürstenkongress) was convened here, attended by Tsar Alexander I of Russia and most German princes of the Confederation of the Rhine, making the city the absolute center of European politics for two weeks. During this time, Napoleon also met the great writer Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, famously greeting him with "Vous êtes un homme" (You are a man).

French rule brought heavy political and financial burdens upon the city. To celebrate the birth of Napoleon's son, French authorities ordered a 20-meter-high Napoleonic column to be erected on Anger Square, with costs footed by the municipal treasury. Napoleon's birthday celebrations were lavish, featuring performances of Haydn's *The Creation* in church and the construction of a rotunda temple housing a bust of Napoleon on a hilltop outside the city.

Following the disastrous defeat of French forces in Russia in 1812, the situation reversed rapidly. Citizens rose up against French rule, and following the Battle of Leipzig in October 1813, Allied forces of Prussia, Austria, and Russia surrounded the city. On January 6, 1814, Allied troops entered the city to cheering crowds, and angry citizens immediately torn down the Napoleonic column symbolizing oppression. On May 7 of the same year, the final 1,700 French troops evacuated Petersberg Citadel. At the Congress of Vienna in 1815, Erfurt was formally restored to Prussia, becoming the capital of a regierungsbezirk (administrative region) in the Prussian Province of Saxony.

Although renewed Prussian rule resulted in the closure of the University of Erfurt, the city gained municipal autonomy in 1822 and successfully transformed its economy. Starting in the 1820s, renowned horticulturists and seed companies were established—such as Haage, Schmidt, and Benary—pioneering illustrated seed catalogs and exporting Erfurt seeds and plants worldwide, earning the city the title of Europe's gardening capital. Industrially, the arrival of the railway in 1847 provided powerful momentum for industrialization. As ancient city walls, gates, and fortifications were progressively demolished between 1874 and 1900, Erfurt expanded rapidly, building new residential quarters and factories. Thanks to swift industrial and population growth, Erfurt passed the 100,000 population mark in 1906, officially joining the ranks of major cities.

### Return to Capital of Thuringia

The two World Wars of the 20th century brought immense disaster to the city. Near the end of World War II, multiple Allied air raids cost many civilian lives and destroyed numerous precious historical buildings and monuments. American forces occupied Erfurt in April 1945 before handing it over to the Soviet Union in July, making it part of East Germany (GDR). During the GDR period, Erfurt lost its status as state capital of Thuringia in 1952 due to administrative boundary reforms, being downgraded to a district capital; yet the courage of its citizens to resist totalitarianism never waned. In the autumn of 1989, a massive peaceful revolution erupted in Erfurt, with tens of thousands of citizens gathering at Cathedral Square to demand democratic reforms, successfully and peacefully occupying the Stasi (State Security) building on December 4.

Following German reunification, Erfurt was reselected as the capital of the State of Thuringia in 1991. The city subsequently embarked on a series of historic revivals: in 1994, the University of Erfurt was re-established after a hiatus of nearly 180 years, and the Catholic Diocese of Erfurt was officially instituted; at the end of 1999, the Federal Labour Court (Bundesarbeitsgericht) officially relocated to Erfurt, boosting its national stature. Entering the 21st century, urban development continues to push forward, combining ancient historical heritage with modern development vitality, firmly anchored at the heart of Germany.


## Erfurt Cathedral (Erfurter Dom)
![Erfurt Dom]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Dom.jpg)
![Erfurt Dom2]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Dom2.jpg)

Forming a medieval architectural masterpiece alongside the adjacent St. Severus Church (St. Severikirche), Erfurt Cathedral is a must-see landmark when visiting the city.

The cathedral's history is full of twists and turns. Its origins trace back to 742 AD when Saint Boniface founded the Diocese of Erfurt. The original small church collapsed in 1153, but construction of a three-aisled Romanesque church began the following year and was completed in 1182. Although this Romanesque structure suffered subsequent modifications, its original charm can still be glimpsed in the walls of the towers today.

After the 14th century, to overcome the steep hillside terrain and build a grander Gothic sanctuary, architects constructed massive vaulted substructures (Cavate) to solve the land constraint. This paved the way for the foundation of the 25-meter-high Gothic choir in 1349. Considered a pinnacle of German Gothic architecture, its walls are almost entirely replaced by 15 soaring stained-glass windows. Sunlight filtering through red, blue, yellow, and green glass illuminates the interior, creating a breathtakingly sacred atmosphere. Remarkably, 12 of these windows retain their original glass from the 1380s to the early 15th century, capturing biblical stories alongside vivid details of contemporary architecture, clothing, weaponry, and handicrafts.

Another highlight is the famous bell, Gloriosa, hanging in the central tower. This giant bell is the world's largest medieval free-swinging bell, measuring over 2.5 meters in diameter at the rim and weighing 11.3 tons, renowned worldwide for its beautiful tone. In 2004, after a fine hairline crack appeared on the bell body, experts undertook a monumental effort to lower it from the tower for specialized welding repairs, returning its heavenly voice to the skies above the city by the end of that year.

## Merchants' Bridge (Krämerbrücke)
![Krämerbrücke]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Krämerbrücke.jpg)

The Krämerbrücke (Merchants' Bridge) is the longest bridge in Europe that is continuously lined with inhabited buildings. It is not only a cultural monument but also the most iconic landmark of Erfurt.

Prior to the 11th century, wooden bridges already spanned the ford of the Gera River. It was first mentioned in documents in 1156 as *pons rerum venalium* (bridge of market goods), featuring wooden stalls and shops. Early wooden structures were repeatedly destroyed by fires until 1325, when the Erfurt city council acquired the site and built a six-arched sandstone stone bridge. Following the great city fire of 1472, the bridge was widened to 26 meters, and half-timbered residential houses were constructed, allowing merchants to settle directly on the bridge. Between the 16th and 18th centuries, space on the bridge underwent multiple renovations, consolidating 62 narrow houses into 32 larger homes. With the city's economic decline after the 17th century, prosperous merchant trade waned, replaced by artisans moving in to reside and run workshops. In the late 19th century, to accommodate growing urban traffic, the bridge faced threats of demolition, saved only by fierce opposition from citizens. Today, the Krämerbrücke Foundation oversees the maintenance and commercial management of this historic structure.

Lined with craft shops and specialty boutiques, walking across the bridge offers a rich sense of history and medieval commercial bustle. Unlike commercial streets dominated by chain stores, every small shop here possesses unique character. For those interested in traditional craftsmanship, the traditional blue-dye workshop (Erfurter Blau) and delicate wooden puppets at Theatrum Mundi are well worth exploring. When tired, stop by the renowned Goldhelm ice cream shop! The variety of flavors induces decision paralysis, and every option looks delicious. Most uniquely, there is even a shop dedicated to tools for left-handed people, where surprising specialty products await. If you prefer slow-paced travel and discovering local creative crafts among ancient architecture, the bridge's blend of history and warmth will surely captivate you.

## St. Aegidius' Church (Ägidienkirche)
![Ägidienkirche]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Ägidienkirche.jpg)

Standing at the eastern entrance of the Krämerbrücke, it is impossible not to be drawn to the Ägidienkirche. First built in 1110, this ancient structure originally served traveling merchants. Its ground-floor archway seamlessly integrates into the bridge, forming the gateway to the Krämerbrücke.

Throughout history, this building served diverse functions. In the Middle Ages, the ground floor featured an open hall used by merchants for trading and shop space. Patronage of the church changed hands multiple times, belonging at points to the Scottish Monastery and local merchant guilds. After 1615, religious services ceased, and parts of the building served as storage space; in the 1920s, it even housed rental apartments. It was not until 1956 that the Methodist Church (now Evangelisch-methodistische Kirche) acquired the property, restored it, and reopened it as a place of worship.

Travel tip: Don't just take photos at the entrance! Be sure to climb the church tower to enjoy a panoramic view overlooking the entire Krämerbrücke and the Erfurt cityscape.

## Petersberg Citadel (Zitadelle Petersberg)
![Zitadelle Petersberg]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/EF-Zitadelle Petersberg.jpg)

Rising above Erfurt's old town, Petersberg Citadel is Central Europe's largest and only fully preserved star-shaped fortress. In 1060, the hill housed only a monastery; however, after Petersberg was incorporated into expanded city fortifications in 1168, its military importance grew. Following his conquest of Erfurt in 1665, the Archbishop of Mainz ordered the construction of a proper fortress. Between 1707 and 1726, master architect Maximilian von Welsch executed massive expansions, turning it into one of the most modern fortresses of its era.

The upheavals of the 19th century brought many changes. During French occupation (1806–1813), Napoleon inspected the site and reinforced its defenses, though subsequent fighting left fortress buildings heavily damaged. Prussian forces regained control in 1814, expanding it between 1815 and 1866 according to the Neo-Prussian defensive system, until Emperor Wilhelm I ordered its demilitarization in 1873, dismantling certain facilities. Military use of Petersberg Citadel continued until 1963. Since 1990, Erfurt has invested significant energy and resources into restoring and preserving this crucial historic site.

Stepping inside Petersberg Citadel, visitors can venture into hidden subterranean tunnels within thick walls, view exhibitions in the commander's quarters, or examine ruins of a medieval Romanesque church, experiencing a fortress whose architecture astonished even Goethe. Furthermore, its unique star-shaped layout and elevated position make it the ultimate vantage point for overlooking Erfurt's old town. Starting from Cathedral Square, whether strolling up along the panoramic path or taking the glass wall elevator to the summit, it is well worth a visit.

## Bernd das Brot / Sendung mit der Maus
![Bernd das Brot]({{ site.baseurl }}/_image/Erfurt/Bernd das Brot.jpg)

Walking through the streets of Erfurt, you might be delighted around any corner by cute animal sculptures. These are no ordinary street art—they represent iconic stars from famous German children's TV shows, such as core characters from *Die Sendung mit der Maus*, *Sandmännchen*, *Siebenstein*, and *Tigerenten Club*. They gather in Erfurt because the headquarters of KiKA, Germany's public children's television channel, is located here, making these character figures natural ambassadors for the city.

Among all the sculptures, the most humorous is undoubtedly Bernd das Brot, located next to the Town Hall. He is essentially a loaf of bread with arms, legs, and a perpetually depressed expression. Although created for a children's channel, his sarcastic, complaining personality made him immensely popular among adults, originally airing during late-night slots. Even crazier, this statue was actually kidnapped once, disappearing for several days before police put in immense effort to recover him—a story arguably even more dramatic than the show itself!
Next time you visit Erfurt, don't forget to hunt for these adorable characters hidden in corners across the city!

## Erfurt Must-Eats

### Wirtshaus Christoffel

The most memorable dining experience in Erfurt is the medieval restaurant Wirtshaus Christoffel. The interior boasts incredible atmosphere, with menus styled to look like parchment paper. Highlights include ribs with a free refill option, paired with rich honey beer. Feasting on meat and beer here is an absolute delight!
